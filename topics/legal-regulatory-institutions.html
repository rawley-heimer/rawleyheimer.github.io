Legal and regulatory institutions

How courts, regulators, and politics shape credit markets and what consumers get from them.

This research shows that more predictable contract enforcement strengthens local credit markets and raises incomes, and that growing up with little access to local banks has lasting effects on credit outcomes, using the assignment of state rather than tribal courts on some Native American reservations as a source of variation in local financial development. It also shows that the prospect of political relief from regulators leads banks to cut lending to protected communities, and that CFPB monitoring of complaint handling raises restitution, especially for higher-income, more educated consumers.

Related terms: law and finance, legal institutions, contract enforcement, courts, regulatory enforcement, political economy, Senate committees, fair-lending enforcement, CFPB supervision, consumer complaints, financial development, economic growth, Public Law 280, Native American reservations.

Published papers

  1. Courting Economic Development

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson

    World Bank Economic Review

    Uncertain court enforcement holds back long-run development: similar reservations under state courts grew faster than those under tribal courts.

  2. Politicizing Consumer Credit

    With Pat Akey and Stefan Lewellen

    Journal of Financial Economics

    Expected political interference weakens regulation: when a state’s senator becomes a powerful committee chair, credit to the state’s minority neighborhoods falls by 7.5%, driven by banks headquartered in the state.

  3. Growing Up Without Finance

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson

    Journal of Financial Economics

    People who grow up with less access to local banks enter credit markets later and, as adults, have lower credit scores and more delinquent accounts.

  4. Law and Finance Matter: Lessons from Externally Imposed Courts

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson

    Review of Financial Studies

    More predictable contract enforcement produces stronger local credit markets for small businesses and households, and higher incomes.

Working papers

  1. Fallible Firms: The Economics of Consumer Redress

    With Charlotte Haendler

    In CFPB complaint data, whether consumers get money back after complaining about a financial firm depends more on who complains than on which firm is involved.
