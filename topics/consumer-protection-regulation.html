Consumer protection and financial regulation

How regulation, supervision, and politics affect people who use financial services.

This research evaluates limits on retail investors’ leverage, which reduced trader losses without harming market liquidity; the Credit CARD Act’s age restrictions on credit cards, which improved long-run credit outcomes at the cost of more mortgage delinquency; and consumer complaints to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, where restitution depends more on who complains than on which firm is involved. It also documents how political power and gerrymandering weaken credit access and fair-lending enforcement.

Related terms: consumer financial protection, CFPB, consumer complaint database, restitution, consumer redress, supervision and enforcement, leverage limits, Dodd-Frank, CFTC, Credit CARD Act, fair lending, disparate treatment, fixed brokerage commissions, political economy of regulation.

Published papers

  1. Politicizing Consumer Credit

    With Pat Akey and Stefan Lewellen

    Journal of Financial Economics

    Expected political interference weakens regulation: when a state’s senator becomes a powerful committee chair, credit to the state’s minority neighborhoods falls by 7.5%, driven by banks headquartered in the state.

  2. Biased by Choice: How Financial Constraints Can Reduce Financial Mistakes

    With Alex Imas

    Review of Financial Studies

    Constraints can reduce financial mistakes: leverage limits improved traders’ performance by curbing the disposition effect, in retail trading data and in two experiments.

  3. Should Retail Investors’ Leverage Be Limited?

    With Alp Simsek

    Journal of Financial Economics

    Limiting retail investors’ access to leverage cut high-leverage traders’ losses by 40% without reducing market liquidity, based on the 2010 U.S. leverage cap in the retail forex market.

Working papers

  1. Fallible Firms: The Economics of Consumer Redress

    With Charlotte Haendler

    In CFPB complaint data, whether consumers get money back after complaining about a financial firm depends more on who complains than on which firm is involved.

  2. Temporal Focal Points and Economic Outcomes: Evidence from U.S. Mortgage Lending

    With Marco Giacoletti and Edison Yu

    Racial disparities in mortgage approvals emerge partly from lender behavior: when month-end quotas push loan officers to approve more loans, the unexplained 7-point Black approval gap shrinks by at least half.

  3. Pushing Boundaries: Political Redistricting and Consumer Credit

    With Pat Akey, Christine Dobridge, and Stefan Lewellen

    Consumers lose access to credit when their congressional districts are gerrymandered.

  4. Brokerage Commissions and Trading Volume: Lessons from the 20th Century

    With Valeria Fedyk

    Draft available on request

    Higher trading costs can increase trading: under fixed NYSE commission schedules, higher commissions raised volume, consistent with brokers steering investors toward high-commission stocks.

  5. Borrowing Young, Paying Later

    With Allison Cole and Igor Livshits

    Draft available on request

    Restricting young adults’ credit access protects them from early defaults but creates adverse selection later: after the Credit CARD Act, credit scores and homeownership rose by age 30 while mortgage delinquency increased.
