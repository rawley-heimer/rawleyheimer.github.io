Research

My research spans household finance, behavioral economics, consumer credit, and financial institutions. I use high-quality microeconomic data, such as credit bureau records, brokerage accounts, and administrative datasets, to study how individuals and institutions make financial decisions and what the consequences are for welfare and policy.

Each paper has its own page with the abstract, a plain-language summary, a study-at-a-glance table, and citation data. Download citations for every paper (BibTeX).

Research topics

  • Retail investing and brokerage7 papers

    How individual investors behave in financial markets, why they so often trade against their own interests, and how leverage limits, brokerage practices, and social networks change their outcomes.

  • Consumer credit and debt8 papers

    How households get, use, and repay credit, and how early-life conditions, cash windfalls, regulation, and politics shape credit outcomes, studied mostly with individual credit bureau records.

  • Mortgage lending and housing5 papers

    Who gets mortgage credit and on what terms, including tests for disparate treatment in lending, the politics of fair-lending enforcement, and the role of institutional investors in single-family housing.

  • Consumer protection and financial regulation7 papers

    How regulation, supervision, and politics affect people who use financial services, from leverage limits for retail traders and age limits on credit cards to complaint resolution at the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

  • Saving, retirement, and inheritance7 papers

    How people accumulate, spend down, and pass on wealth over their lives: saving for retirement, beliefs about longevity, bequests and inheritances, private family foundations, and the effects of wealth shocks.

  • Behavioral finance and beliefs9 papers

    Why people’s financial choices depart from what standard models predict: biased beliefs about risk and longevity, plans that change once outcomes arrive, social influence among investors, and how generative AI may change financial decisions.

  • Legal institutions and financial development3 papers

    How courts and contract enforcement shape credit markets and long-run economic development, studied through the assignment of state versus tribal courts on Native American reservations.

Published papers

  1. Dynamic Inconsistency in Risky Choice: Evidence from the Lab and Field

    With Zwetelina Iliewa, Alex Imas, and Martin Weber. American Economic Review.

    People plan to keep taking risk after gains and stop after losses, but in practice they cash out gains early and chase losses.

  2. Shale Shocked: Cash Windfalls and Household Debt Repayment

    With J. Anthony Cookson and Erik P. Gilje. Journal of Financial Economics.

    Households that receive cash windfalls mostly pay down debt, and the effect is three times larger for subprime borrowers than for prime borrowers.

  3. Biased by Choice: How Financial Constraints Can Reduce Financial Mistakes

    With Alex Imas. Review of Financial Studies.

    Limits on borrowing can make investors better off by curbing their behavioral biases.

  4. Personal Wealth, Self-Employment, and Business Ownership

    With Aymeric Bellon, J. Anthony Cookson, and Erik P. Gilje. Review of Financial Studies.

    Large cash windfalls raise business formation but do not make people more likely to become self-employed.

  5. Politicizing Consumer Credit

    With Pat Akey and Stefan Lewellen. Journal of Financial Economics.

    When a state’s senator becomes a powerful committee chair, credit access in the state’s minority neighborhoods falls by 7.5%.

  6. YOLO: Mortality Beliefs and Household Finance Puzzles

    With Kristian Ove R. Myrseth and Raphael S. Schoenle. Journal of Finance.

    Young people underestimate their chances of survival and older people overestimate theirs, which helps explain under-saving and slow retirement drawdown.

  7. Should Retail Investors’ Leverage Be Limited?

    With Alp Simsek. Journal of Financial Economics.

    Capping how much leverage retail traders can use made them better off without harming market liquidity.

  8. Growing Up Without Finance

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson. Journal of Financial Economics.

    People who grow up without local banks enter credit markets later and, as adults, have lower credit scores and more delinquent accounts.

  9. Law and Finance Matter: Lessons from Externally Imposed Courts

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson. Review of Financial Studies.

    Reservations assigned to state courts, which enforce contracts more predictably, have stronger credit markets and higher incomes.

  10. Peer Pressure: Social Interaction and the Disposition Effect

    Review of Financial Studies.

    Gaining access to a social trading network nearly doubles a trader’s tendency to sell winners and hold on to losers.

  11. Financial Decision Making, at the Dawn of AI

    Oxford Research Encyclopedia of Economics and Finance, forthcoming.

    Reviews why people’s saving, investing, and borrowing choices depart from what financial economics prescribes, and how generative AI might change them.

  12. Subjective Beliefs, Saving, and Spending for Retirement

    Chapter in Retirement Saving, Investment, and Spending: New Lessons from Behavioral Research.

    Reviews how beliefs about the future, especially about one’s own survival, change the predictions of standard models of retirement saving and spending.

  13. Courting Economic Development

    With James R. Brown and J. Anthony Cookson. World Bank Economic Review.

    Reservations under state courts grew faster over the long run than similar reservations whose tribal courts enforced contracts less predictably.

  14. Friends Do Let Friends Buy Stocks Actively

    Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization.

    Social interaction is more common among active traders than among passive investors.

  15. Facebook Finance: How Social Interaction Propagates Active Investing

    With David Simon. Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland Working Paper 15-22.

    Active trading strategies spread through social networks of retail traders, even though the network reveals how rarely they succeed.

Working papers

  1. Temporal Focal Points and Economic Outcomes: Evidence from U.S. Mortgage Lending

    With Marco Giacoletti and Edison Yu. Working paper.

    Loan officers’ month-end volume quotas account for at least half of the unexplained racial gap in mortgage approvals, pointing to lender behavior.

  2. Fallible Firms: The Economics of Consumer Redress

    With Charlotte Haendler. Working paper.

    Whether consumers get money back after complaining about a financial firm depends more on who complains than on which firm is involved.

  3. Pushing Boundaries: Political Redistricting and Consumer Credit

    With Pat Akey, Christine Dobridge, and Stefan Lewellen. Working paper.

    Consumers lose access to credit when their congressional districts are gerrymandered.

  4. Five Facts about Bequest Motives

    With Xiang Li. Working paper.

    Bequest motives are stronger for people with heirs and the very wealthy, follow a U-shape over life, and people overestimate the bequests they will leave.

  5. The Wealth Draw-down of the Fabulously Rich: Insights from Private Family Foundations

    With Changhyun Ahn. Draft available on request.

    When the eldest member of a very wealthy family dies, the family foundation roughly doubles in size, but charitable giving lags far behind.

  6. Single-Family REITs and Local Housing Markets

    With Marco Giacoletti, Wenli Li, and Edison Yu. Working paper.

    Single-family REITs buy homes in neighborhoods ringing city centers, and their growth shows little evidence of harming local housing markets.

  7. Brokerage Commissions and Trading Volume: Lessons from the 20th Century

    With Valeria Fedyk. Draft available on request.

    Under fixed NYSE commission schedules, higher commissions led to more trading, consistent with brokers steering investors toward high-commission stocks.

  8. Borrowing Young, Paying Later

    With Allison Cole and Igor Livshits. Draft available on request.

    The Credit CARD Act’s limits on cards for people under 21 raised credit scores and homeownership by age 30 but increased mortgage delinquency.

  9. Waiting for Wealth: Inheritances and Household Savings

    With Eirik Eylands Brandsaas and Mateo Velásquez-Giraldo. Draft available on request.

    Expected inheritances raise consumption and reduce saving long before they arrive, and leaving them out makes standard models overstate how patient households are.

Guides and policy writing

  1. Navigating Credit Bureau Data: Field Notes for Researchers

    Research guide.

    A practical guide to using credit bureau data in research: what it can measure, its limitations, how to acquire it, and how to merge it with other data.

  2. Are People Overconfident about Avoiding COVID-19?

    With Haoyang Liu and Xiaohan Zhang. Liberty Street Economics, Federal Reserve Bank of New York.

    People expect their own exposure to COVID-19 to be well below what they expect for the general public.

  3. Intergenerational Homeownership and Mortgage Distress

    With Nicholas Fritsch. Economic Commentary, Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland.

    Parents’ mortgage distress during the 2008 crisis is associated with a lower chance that their children buy a home.

  4. Geographic Mobility and Consumer Financial Health: Evidence from Oil Production Boom Towns

    With Timothy Stehulak and Caitlin Treanor. Economic Commentary, Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland.

    Residents who move away from oil boom towns after the bust end up with stronger consumer financial health than those who stay.
