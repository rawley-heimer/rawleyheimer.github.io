Research › Consumer credit and debt

Journal of Financial Economics 146 (3): 905–931

Shale Shocked: Cash Windfalls and Household Debt Repayment

J. Anthony Cookson, Erik P. Gilje, and Rawley Z. Heimer

Abstract. Using individual credit bureau data matched with cash windfalls from fracking, we estimate that windfall recipients reduce debt-to-income by 2.4 percentage points relative to no-windfall controls. Debt repayment effects are 3 times stronger for subprime individuals than for prime individuals. Based on the timing of upfront versus continuing cash payments, debt repayment coincides with the timing of payments but not with news about future payments. These findings present a challenge for purely forward-looking models of debt. Indeed, when we incorporate a windfall shock into a forward-looking model, the model predicts an increase in debt that runs counter to our evidence of debt repayment.

Keywords: income shocks, liquidity constraints, marginal propensity to consume, cash windfalls, household debt, debt repayment, fracking, shale royalties, credit bureau data, subprime borrowers, permanent income hypothesis.

JEL codes: D14, D15, G51, N52.

In brief

When households receive a sudden cash windfall, many use it to pay down debt rather than borrow more, a pattern that purely forward-looking models of household debt struggle to explain.

Matching individual credit bureau records to royalty payments from shale oil and gas drilling (fracking), the paper finds that windfall recipients cut their debt-to-income ratio by 2.4 percentage points relative to similar people who received nothing, and the effect is three times larger for subprime borrowers than for prime borrowers. Repayment tracks when the cash actually arrives, not when people learn that future payments are coming. That pattern is hard to square with purely forward-looking models of borrowing, which predict that debt should rise after good news about future income.

Contributes to research on household debt and deleveraging, household responses to income and wealth shocks, liquidity constraints, and the permanent income hypothesis.
