Research › Saving, retirement, and inheritance

Journal of Finance 74 (6): 2957–2996

YOLO: Mortality Beliefs and Household Finance Puzzles

Rawley Z. Heimer, Kristian Ove R. Myrseth, and Raphael S. Schoenle

Dimensional Fund Advisors Distinguished Paper Award. TIAA Paul A. Samuelson Award Finalist.

Abstract. We study the effect of subjective mortality beliefs on life-cycle behavior. With new survey evidence, we document that survival is underestimated by the young and overestimated by the old. We calibrate a canonical life-cycle model to elicited beliefs. Relative to calibrations using actuarial probabilities, the young under-save by 26%, and retirees draw down their assets 27% slower, while the model’s fit to consumption data improves by 88%. Cross-sectional regressions support the model’s predictions: distorted mortality beliefs correlate with savings behavior while controlling for risk preferences, cognitive, and socioeconomic factors. Overweighting the likelihood of rare events contributes to mortality belief distortions.

Keywords: time discounting, time preference, discount rates, consumption puzzles, subjective mortality beliefs, life expectancy, life-cycle model, retirement saving, undersaving, asset decumulation, probability weighting, survey evidence.

In brief

Beliefs about survival act like time discounting in the life-cycle model: because young people underestimate and older people overestimate their chances of living, the young save too little and retirees spend down their savings too slowly.

New survey evidence shows that young people underestimate their chances of survival while older people overestimate theirs. Feeding these beliefs into a standard life-cycle model, the young under-save by 26% and retirees draw down assets 27% more slowly than they would using actuarial survival odds, and the model’s fit to real consumption data improves by 88%. The distortions are linked to overweighting the likelihood of rare events.

Contributes to research on time discounting and time preference, life-cycle saving and consumption puzzles, retirement saving and decumulation, and subjective expectations.
