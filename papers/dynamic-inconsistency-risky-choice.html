American Economic Review 115 (1): 330–363

Dynamic Inconsistency in Risky Choice: Evidence from the Lab and Field

Rawley Z. Heimer, Zwetelina Iliewa, Alex Imas, and Martin Weber

Abstract. We document a robust dynamic inconsistency in risky choice. Using a unique brokerage dataset and a series of experiments, we compare people’s initial risk-taking plans to their subsequent decisions. Across settings, people accept risk as part of a “loss-exit” strategy — planning to continue taking risk after gains and stopping after losses. Actual behavior deviates from initial strategies by cutting gains early and chasing losses. More people accept risk when offered a commitment to their initial strategy. Our results help reconcile seemingly contradictory findings on risk-taking in static versus dynamic contexts. We explore implications for theory and welfare.

Keywords: dynamic inconsistency, risk-taking, loss chasing, realization of gains, commitment devices, prospect theory, brokerage data, lab experiments.

In brief

People’s plans for taking risk differ systematically from what they actually do once outcomes arrive.

Using brokerage account data and a series of experiments, the paper shows that people typically plan to keep taking risk after gains and to stop after losses, but in practice they do the reverse: they cash out gains early and keep chasing losses. When offered a way to commit to their original plan, more people are willing to take the risk in the first place. The findings help reconcile why studies of one-shot gambles and studies of ongoing, repeated risk-taking often reach different conclusions about people’s appetite for risk.
