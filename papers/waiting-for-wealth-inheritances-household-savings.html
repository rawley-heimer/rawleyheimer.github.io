Waiting for Wealth: Inheritances and Household Savings

Eirik Eylands Brandsaas, Rawley Z. Heimer, and Mateo Velásquez-Giraldo

Abstract. Inheritances account for a quarter to a half of U.S. household wealth, yet the canonical life-cycle model of saving omits them. We extend the model so that households have a living parent whose wealth evolves stochastically and who leaves an inheritance at death, and we calibrate parental mortality, parental wealth dynamics, and inheritance slippage using the SCF, PSID, and HRS. Inheritances generate large, positive income shocks whose probability, size, and timing are all uncertain. Omitting them leads to an overestimate of the discount factor (0.947 versus 0.933), a difference that compounds substantially over life-cycle horizons. Anticipated inheritances raise the level and dispersion of consumption at all ages, lower saving among younger households, and increase the dispersion of wealth before about age 60. Consistent with the model, households with wealthier parents consume more, all else equal. Households would pay substantial amounts to receive their inheritance today, particularly when their parents are wealthy.

Keywords: time discounting, income risk, inheritances, bequests, life-cycle model, household saving, consumption, wealth distribution, discount factor, parental wealth, intergenerational transfers.

In brief

Leaving inheritances out of the life-cycle model makes households look more patient than they are; expected inheritances change saving long before the money arrives.

The paper adds inheritances to a standard life-cycle model of saving: households have a living parent whose wealth changes over time and who leaves an inheritance at death, with parental mortality, wealth, and the share lost to taxes and fees calibrated from the SCF, PSID, and HRS. Inheritances act as large, positive income shocks whose size, probability, and timing are all uncertain. Leaving them out makes the model overstate how patient households are. Including them raises consumption and its spread at all ages, mostly through anticipation, and widens differences in wealth among younger households. Households would pay substantial sums to receive their inheritance today, especially when their parents are wealthy.

Contributes to research on life-cycle saving and consumption, estimating time discounting, income risk, and inheritances and the distribution of wealth.
